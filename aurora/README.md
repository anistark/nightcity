# Aurora

A low rise of thin snow above a wide river, looking up. Night City and far mountains sit along the horizon, and about seventy percent of the view is sky. Curtains of aurora fold, curl, surge and fade across part of it, the stars turn slowly behind them, and the river and the snow take on their light, while a boat comes past now and then on the current.

The dance has no pattern to repeat. An auroral curtain is a thin sheet of charge lying along the magnetic field, and it moves the way a vortex sheet moves in a flat fluid: it folds, rolls up into curls and spirals, and unwinds. So each curtain here is a chain of points carried by a flow. The flow is made of seeded vortices that are born, travel and die on their own timers, on top of slow shear whose strength wanders. Stretching and folding like this is chaotic, so small differences grow and the shapes never come round again. Points are added where a sheet stretches and removed where it bunches up, and a little tension relaxes kinks while the large folds stay.

A substorm cycle sets the mood. Quiet arcs lie across the sky, then a slow build brightens them and draws them closer. A breakup throws out new curtains, rolls great folds along them and spreads them across the sky, and sometimes a corona opens high above, with rays running together at one point. The recovery leaves soft patches that pulse on periods of their own. Each stage lasts an exponentially distributed time, and how strong tonight's storms run is seeded.

The aurora takes part of the sky and leaves the rest to the stars. A soft boundary wanders round the top and the sides of the view over a few minutes, curtains fade out as they near it, and a storm eats into the open part without closing it. Only a corona ignores it. The boundary never reaches the bottom of the view, and one curtain always sits low, so the river has something to hold.

A curtain is a continuous glow first. A few broad rays lift softly out of it, each with its own place, width, strength and lifetime, and many places hold none, so they fall in loose groups with long gaps. Trains of small curls run along a sheet, brightenings race down it, and sometimes light flames upward through the rays. Far off, rays blur together through hundreds of kilometres of air, so a distant curtain reads as a smooth band.

The light is worked out from what the atoms do. Sheets are drawn onto a map of the field lines around the viewer, in polar form around the magnetic zenith, with angle across and the log of distance up. A line of sight then runs straight along one column of that map, so each pixel walks up a column adding what every altitude emits. Hard electrons reach down to about 95 kilometres and light a pink lower edge from nitrogen, middling ones give the main green of oxygen near 120 kilometres, and soft ones stay high and bring the red of oxygen above 200 kilometres, mixed with a little blue from nitrogen. Perspective, rays meeting at the zenith, curtains brightening where they fold edge on, and arcs that stay above the horizon because the Earth curves away below them all come out of that walk with nothing faked. The map keeps a memory too, because the atoms do: green lingers for a fraction of a second, and the red glow lasts most of a minute after the sheet that lit it has moved on, drifting outward as it fades.

The sky behind it is real. The hundred or so brightest stars are in their true places, so Orion, the Plough, Cassiopeia and the rest turn across the view, at four times the real rate so the turning shows if you stay a while. Eighty thousand fainter stars crowd toward the plane of the galaxy, and each has a colour from its temperature. Stars twinkle most near the horizon, where their light crosses the most air, and dim and redden there too. The Milky Way is baked once onto the celestial sphere, with star clouds, soft dust lanes and a warm core, so it rises and sets with the stars and some hours it isn't there at all. Faint banded airglow ripples high up, high cloud drifts in and out, dark against the aurora and lit by the city from below, and each night has its own moon or none. Meteors, satellites and planes pass on their own timers.

The land is drawn once for each screen size and lit every frame. The near snow is a height map, marched per pixel, thin enough that dark ground comes through where the wind has scoured it and dry grass stands up through it in clumps. Spruce stand along the ridge, two ranges of mountains lie far off, and Night City rises between them in three depths of towers with lit windows, neon, slow red beacons and flying cars. Snow under an open sky is lit by all of it at once, so it takes its light from the whole sky as it is right now and brightens and tints with the storm, while the snow nearest the horizon picks up the city's glow.

The river runs across the view below the viewer's low rise, open water early in the winter, with long bends, thin ice along the banks and sedge at the water's edge. It mirrors the last finished frame, so the aurora, the city, the stars and the moon all land in it, strongest toward the far bank, where the water is seen at the flattest angle. That bank is marched once with the land, so the water near it shows the snow above it. Ripples and pans of new ice ride the current, which runs one way or the other each night. The wind comes and goes on smooth noise: ripples break the reflection into streaks, and when the wind drops the water runs smooth as glass.

A boat comes past on the current every few minutes, one at a time, on an exponential timer. Each is painted fresh from its own stream: a rowing boat with a hooded figure, an empty one, or one with a small lit cabin, in a length and shape of its own, most with a lamp and some with a line of neon in the night's colours. It stands on the water as a picture, passing side on, and bobs and rolls a little. The water works out where each reflected line of sight meets that picture, so the boat lands in the river under itself, ripples and all, and its lamp draws a tall streak down the water.

Open `/aurora/` with the local server running, or `/aurora/embed/` for the scene alone. The shared overlay supplies the clock, seed log, author credit, share and fullscreen controls. Press L for the log, H to hide the overlay and F for fullscreen. Counters track substorms, curtains, coronas, boats, meteors and satellites.

The renderer is WebGL 2, in `assets/js/gl.js`, because a volume of light can't be walked per pixel in Canvas 2D. `assets/js/main.js` holds what both renderers share, the seeded night, the simulation, the stars and the painting of the city, trees and grass, and ends with a simpler Canvas 2D version: the same simulated curtains drawn as soft wide strokes of light up each field line, turned over into the river, with the same boats. It runs when WebGL 2, half float buffers or a shader fail. `?2d` forces it and `?glfail` makes WebGL fail on purpose.

A frame is a chain of passes. Sheets go onto the map as strips of triangles, with their rays worked out per pixel, and the map eases toward them. The walk up the columns runs at a resolution that adapts to the machine, and `?aq=0.7` fixes it. High cloud and airglow run at a quarter of the resolution. Then everything is added up in linear light in half floats, the stars and other lights are drawn as small Gaussians, a bloom chain stands in for the glow of the air and the lens, its first level is kept for the river to mirror next time, and the last pass tones the picture and dithers it, since a dark sky bands easily. `?glq=0.7` lowers the whole picture's resolution. Baking happens a band at a time and nothing is shown until the whole night is ready, then it opens slowly. Reduced motion shows one still from a sky that has already been running. Embeds run smaller and at about 30 frames a second.

One clock-seeded generator drives the aurora. The land, the city and the stars each take a private stream from that seed, so painting them never changes what the sky does, and drawing, resizing and quality settings consume no random values.

With `?debug`, `window.AU` exposes `pause()`, `resume()`, `run(seconds)`, `step(seconds)`, `draw()`, `fire(event)`, `state()`, `clouds(cover)`, `cover(level)`, which gives the share of the sky in view that the aurora lights, `profile()`, which times each stage of a frame, `renderer()` and `map(channel, gain)`, which returns the emission map as an image. Events are `curl`, `train`, `surge`, `pulse`, `flame`, `arc`, `split`, `corona`, `boat`, `meteor`, `satellite`, `plane`, `car` and `cloud`. `?speed=8` accelerates playback.
