# Snow Bubble

A soap bubble comes down close to the lens on a snowfield below moonlit mountains, freezes from the snow or from flakes that land on it, turns white under layer after layer of frost, and then bursts into thin curved flakes that flutter down into the snow. Other bubbles fall far off across the field, each going the same way in its own time.

The night comes first. The whole scene is there from the first frame, with the snowfield, the range, the trees and the sky opening together, and the bubble follows a few seconds later. Each bubble arrives its own way. Its direction, height, speed and path are all seeded, from a low run in along the snow to almost straight down. It comes down at its own spot, close enough to the lens to fill most of the frame, and meets the snow at its own speed. A slow bubble seats itself with barely a sound. A faster one gives, pushes out a breath of powder, skips on a little way and leaves a dimple at each touch. One with sideways speed rolls to a stop and leaves a faint track. It may rest lightly on top of the snow on a tiny foot or settle deep into it, with the snow hiding its base. Air carries it down over about twelve seconds, and a damped shape spring lets the film give to each touch and to later gusts until the shell stiffens.

The film is a moving layer, not a pattern. Its thickness is carried over the sphere by seeded eddies that wander and change strength, by a slow tumble while the bubble is in the air, by the roll of a landing and by gravity draining it downward. Thick and thin patches keep regenerating, so the stirring never blends it to one tone. Light interfering across that thickness gives the colors, and the near and far walls each reflect the snow and sky, so the bands stretch, fold and slide over one another and never repeat. As the freeze climbs, the swirls stop where they stand.

Ice can start in more than one place. Cold always enters where the film meets the snow, but how fast differs from bubble to bubble, and a snowflake landing on the crown or the side of a bubble can start ice there too, which then spreads outward and down. Some bubbles freeze from one place and some from several. Each start is a cap growing over the sphere, with an uneven, wandering edge, and the slightly raised camera lets the eye read it as travelling around the shell and not as a flat level. Ahead of the ice the film is liquid. At its edge the film dulls to a pale creeping line. Behind it the shell is frosted and scatters what is behind it.

The frost itself is grown, not drawn, and it grows as feathers, the way ice does on a film. A spine runs straight along its crystal axis. Barbs leave it close together and nearly parallel, sixty degrees off, longest through the middle and running out toward the point, so each arm reads as a serrated fern blade with a faint milky body. Along the line where film meets snow the ice stands up in a bright contact line and a fringe of short fronds, and some of those go on to climb. Where a flake landed, a star of spines opens instead. A tip may run a little ahead of the cold and then has to wait for it, so the feathered edge moves as the cold does. A spine that reaches other ice stops and leaves a small bright joint, which is how separate ferns knit into one lace.

No two crystals are the same, and no two bubbles grow the same kind. Snow crystals take their form from temperature and humidity, so each bubble has a crystal weather of its own: how feathered its crystals are, how many arms survive, how often a barb forks into a new arm, how much is bare needle, how large a plate sits at the center. Each crystal then varies continuously within that weather, with its own barb spacing, its own lopsidedness and arms of its own lengths, so the same pattern cannot come round twice. Most crystals on a film are irregular, with one or two long arms and the rest short or missing.

Ahead of the ice, large free crystals nucleate in the film, and snow landing on film close to freezing can seed one too. They begin as faint specks, grow arms and barbs, and turn slowly as they ride one shared current, as described in [How soap bubbles freeze](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-10021-6). The current is deliberately gentle and softly limited, however cold the night. Crystals that come close draw together, as bodies floating on a film do. If they stay in contact a neck of ice forms and they travel on as one piece. When the ice reaches them they lock, their arms become part of the lace, and growth carries on from their tips. Crystals on the far wall are out of the lens's focus and show only as soft grey shapes.

Falling snow that meets the bubble stays on it. On liquid film a grain is carried by the current, eases downhill and slowly melts in. On ice it holds, creeping only where the shell is steep or when a breath of wind nudges it.

The first lace is only the start. More flakes keep opening on ice that is already there, in layers of their own, and each layer scatters more light. White spreads from wherever frost has been growing longest, until the whole shell is opaque. Any gap the lace has grown around gets a crystal of its own, so the shell always closes completely. A mild spell can slow the front but cannot stop it. Nothing can end a bubble before it is enclosed and white, and only then does its seeded failure budget start to run, with wind and temperature swings hurrying it along. There is no fixed lifetime.

The ending is taken from slow footage of a frozen bubble breaking, and from reports that [air leaks out through gaps between the crystals](https://www.popsci.com/how-to-freeze-soap-bubbles-into-ice-orbs/) until the shell [can no longer hold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap_bubble). It holds, and then in a moment a tear races round the shell from one side. The whole thing takes well under a second, and colder shells go faster. What was shell is now thin curved pieces and fine slivers of its own lace, still holding the bubble's outline. Thin ice seen face on is nearly clear and only shines when the eye looks along it, so a broken shell reads as a bright rim around almost nothing, and each piece glints as it turns edge on. The pieces hang for an instant, then flutter down like paper, with a few broad sheets among the small ones. A faint mist stays a little while in the shape of the air the bubble held. Nothing vanishes on the way down. Everything lands where it falls, in a ring around the foot, and is slowly lost to the snow. The rates, rendering and failure are qualitative generative models rather than quantitative predictions of a real bubble.

Open `/snow-bubble/` with the local server running, or `/snow-bubble/embed/` for the scene alone. The shared overlay supplies the clock, seed log, author credit, share and fullscreen controls. Press L for the log, H to hide the overlay and F for fullscreen. Text uses Pixelspace. Counters track bubbles, ice crystals, frozen shells, burst shells and snow leopards.

The land is real terrain, different every night, and the ground and the mountains are one surface. The snow at the bubble's foot and the snow on the peaks come from the same height map, the same moon and the same shading, drawn in one pass, so nothing is laid over anything else. A ridged fractal heightfield is folded and raised until the highest ground reaches a seeded height in the sky. Tens of thousands of simulated water drops then run down it, cutting gullies and leaving branching ridges, and weathering opens those cuts out. Snow fills the hollows. It holds on slopes up to about fifty degrees and leaves dark, banded rock above that, with snow caught on its ledges and runnels streaked down the true fall line of each face. The moon sits behind the camera to the left, low enough that every ridge casts a real shadow, and shade fills in blue from the sky. The whole view is then drawn in perspective, one column at a time, through thin night air, so far ranges pale and the skyline stays soft. The snowfield runs out from the foot of the range in long low drifts. Close to, three sizes of wind crust and ripple, all lying along one seeded wind, turn their faces toward the moon or away from it, and fade out with distance before the pixels can no longer hold them.

Patches of forest climb the foothills and stop at a treeline. Stands of spruce under a winter's load stand on the land itself at the far edge of the snowfield, each at its own distance and size, thick at the sides of the view and thinning toward the middle. Each throws a long moon shadow away across the snow. Everything behind the bubble is drawn a little soft and held below its brightness, the way a lens would, flakes passing close to the lens are large and soft, and single grains on the snow catch the moon in turn.

Other bubbles come down far off on their own exponential timer, at random places across the field and at their own speeds and distances. They stay between the near bubble and the trees, and they show through its film. Each lands on the real ground at that spot, freezes from the base up, turns white and only then breaks into a drift of glints, the same rule the near bubble follows.

Now and then, high on the skyline, a snow leopard comes up over a ridge, stands for a moment against the night, walks a few lengths along it and drops out of sight. It only ever appears on the highest ground, far above the plain, where snow leopards live, and it is a very small thing to see. Most of the time you will miss it, which is the point of a snow leopard.

The sky is deliberately quiet: a sparse field of stars using One Sock's palette and shimmer, with the main stars of Orion, Cassiopeia and Ursa Major's Big Dipper among them and no lines drawn between them. It is not a location-specific astronomical chart. Stars and shooting stars pass behind the mountains.

The moon belongs to the night. Each night has its own, in one fixed place in the sky, and its shape runs anywhere from a thin crescent through half to full. Some nights there is no moon in the sky at all. It never rises, sets or moves. It is a small lit sphere with its own dark seas and a trace of earthshine, and the sky around it glows more as it fills. The light on the snow always comes from behind the camera. A faint aurora comes and goes on its own timer. Its curtain is a row of columns, each with its own lower edge, height and brightness, all slowly moving, green low down and a thin red higher up. It fades in and out over about twenty seconds, stays well under the brightness of the snow, and gives the snowfield a breath of its color.

One shared clock-seeded generator drives every random choice. Independent exponential event budgets control crystal arrivals, new dendrites, frost layers, wind changes, snowfall, shooting stars, snow leopards, background bubbles, the aurora, flakes that start the ice, failure and pauses. Star-field and frost-detail counts are sampled from seeded distributions with no fixed count or upper cutoff. Spatial lookups speed up contact checks without discarding crystals or limiting their number. Drawing, resizing and quality settings consume no random values and do not alter the seeded world. The land takes its own private stream from that generator, so building it never changes what the bubble does.

The scene uses smooth procedural Canvas 2D at device resolution. The land is too much work for one frame, so it is built in large slices while the screen stays dark, which takes about a second. The clock does not run until it is done. Then the finished scene opens all at once, and from there it is one cached image that costs almost nothing to draw. Finished lace is baked into layers for the near and far side of the shell every third frame, so each frame only draws what changed, and a soft blue shadow of it keeps the feathers readable once the shell is white. Crystal branches are mapped point by point onto the sphere, with near and far segments separated at the silhouette. The film's thickness lives on an octahedral map, which wraps the whole sphere onto one square with no pinched poles, and is carried by a sharpened semi-Lagrangian step. The lines the shell will break along are settled while it holds, and the lace each piece carries is copied into one shared image, so the moment of the tear costs almost nothing. The camera accommodates the sampled radius rather than limiting the bubble's size, pans on narrow screens to keep the landing in frame, and eases between bubbles while the landscape stays fixed. Reduced motion selects a still from naturally developed seeded frost. Embeds follow the shared quality, pause/play and recording behavior without an overlay.

With `?debug`, `window.SB` exposes `pause()`, `run()`, `step(seconds)`, `draw()`, `state()` and `fire(event)`. State returns a copy, with the frost reported as counts. Events are `form`, `nucleate`, `collapse`, `current`, `snow`, `meteor`, `leopard`, `drift` and `aurora`, with physically invalid events rejected, so `collapse` is refused until the shell is enclosed and white. Debug events exercise the same processes as the running scene. They do not assign a future outcome. `?speed=8` accelerates playback. Debug pages also expose `render_game_to_text()` and `advanceTime(ms)`.
